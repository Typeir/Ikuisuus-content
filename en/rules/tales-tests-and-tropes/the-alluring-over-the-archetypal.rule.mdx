# The Alluring Over the Archetypal

An archetype is a shape a player already knows. The gruff innkeeper. The corrupt magistrate. The dying mentor. Archetypes are efficient — you say three words and the whole table pictures the same person — and that efficiency is exactly the problem. A table that has already pictured the person has stopped paying attention to them.

Something alluring cannot be pictured in advance. It has one detail that does not fit, and the table leans in to find out why.

---

## The Detail That Does Not Fit

Take the archetype. Keep it. Then break exactly one thing about it.

- The gruff innkeeper who keeps a Binturian lullaby written out above the till, in a hand that is not his.
- The corrupt magistrate who has never once taken coin, only favours, and keeps meticulous records of every one.
- The dying mentor who is not dying, and is furious that everyone has decided she is.

One fracture is enough. Two is a puzzle box; three is noise. The archetype supplies the shorthand so the fracture can carry the meaning, and the players do the rest of the work themselves — which is the point. A character the table theorises about is a character the table cares about.

---

## Want, Cost, Contradiction

For anyone who will appear more than once, know three things before they open their mouth:

- **What do they want?** Concrete and present-tense. Not "power" — *the deed to her brother's foundry, back, before the winter assize.*
- **What has it already cost them?** Desire without a paid price is a plot hook, not a person.
- **Where do they contradict themselves?** People want incompatible things and act on both. The contradiction is where the players get purchase.

Three lines. That is the whole preparation. Anything longer is a backstory you will read to yourself.

---

## Factions Are Characters Too

The same rule scales. A faction described by its function — *the thieves' guild*, *the church*, *the northern clans* — is furniture. A faction described by its appetite is an actor: what is it hungry for, what has it lost, what will it not do even when doing it would win?

Give a faction one policy it holds past the point of self-interest. That is where players find leverage, and where the faction becomes something they can be betrayed by.

---

## Against the Comfortable Read

Damocles is not a setting of good and evil, and archetypes smuggle that framing back in through the side door. The bandit who is *just* a bandit teaches the table that the world sorts cleanly. It does not.

- Let the antagonist be right about something.
- Let the ally be wrong about something that matters.
- Let the monster want a thing a person would want. See [Monster Stat Blocks](/en/library/rules/creatures-critters-and-characters/monster-stat-blocks) for the numbers; the numbers do not tell you what it is protecting.

Where the setting already supplies a specific horror — Nigredo's rot, Xanthous and the miracle, the Bleak Bloom — use it in preference to a generic one. The library is full of things that have never appeared at any other table. Reach for those first: see [The World Is a Character](/en/library/rules/tales-tests-and-tropes/the-world-is-a-character) and the [world library](/en/library/world).

---

## The Test

Before an NPC reaches the table, answer this: **could a player describe them to someone who was not there, in one sentence, without using their job title?**

If not, you have furniture. Furniture is fine — most of a world is furniture, and inns need innkeepers. But every scene that matters needs at least one person in it who fails that test in the other direction: someone the players will still be arguing about three sessions later.
