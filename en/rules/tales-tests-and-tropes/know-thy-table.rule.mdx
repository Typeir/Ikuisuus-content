# Know Thy Table

Damocles is a cruel setting. It has curses that do not lift, wounds that do not close, and an ending already written. None of that is a licence to hurt the people sitting at your table.

The rules in this book describe what the world can do. They do not describe what your group wants to play. Those are different questions, and only one of them has an answer printed in a book. The other you have to ask.

---

## Ask Before You Build

Do this before the first session, not after the first casualty.

- **What is this campaign about?** Survival at the edge of the Hinterlands is a different game from Borossan court intrigue, even with identical rules.
- **How lethal?** Damocles supports character death as a routine outcome. It does not require it. Say which you are running, out loud, before anyone writes a name on a sheet.
- **What is off the table?** Some of what Damocles contains — bodily corruption, the loss of self to madness, children in harm's way, religious violence — will land differently on different people. Ask what nobody wants to encounter. Take the answer at face value and do not ask why.
- **What does each player want to do?** One wants to fight, one wants to talk, one wants to solve the ruin. A campaign that never offers a player their preferred verb is a campaign they are not in.
- **How long, how often?** A twelve-session arc and an open-ended chronicle demand different pacing from the same rules.

Write the answers down. Revisit them. A table's appetite changes over a year, and the only way to find out is to ask again.

---

## The Table Outranks the Book

Every rule in this library is a default, and defaults exist to be replaced by something better suited to the people in the room. Change what does not serve you.

What matters is that the change is **known**. A rule quietly ignored is not a house rule; it is an inconsistency, and inconsistency erodes the trust described in [The Contract of Mechanics](/en/library/rules/tales-tests-and-tropes/the-contract-of-mechanics). Announce alterations before play, not mid-roll.

If your table finds permanent curses miserable rather than tense, make them curable. If your table finds a ten-day [# kw:Recovery #] too slow for a heist campaign, shorten it and accept what that does to attrition. Neither choice is wrong. Both must be stated.

---

## Read the Room While You Run It

The session-zero conversation is not the whole job. People rarely interrupt a scene to say they are uncomfortable; they go quiet, they check out, they start rolling dice for no reason.

- Notice who has not spoken in twenty minutes and give them something only their character can do.
- Notice when a scene has stopped landing and cut it. A scene that ends early costs nothing. A scene that overstays costs the session.
- Give everyone a way to stop play without explanation. A phrase, a card, a raised hand — whatever your group agrees on. Honour it instantly and without discussion, then move the camera. You can ask afterwards, in private, if the person wants to talk about it.
- Check in after heavy sessions. Two minutes at the end is enough.

---

## Cruelty Is a Setting, Not a Style

Damocles is grim because grimness gives small kindnesses weight. See [The Eulogy of Damocles](/en/library/rules/tales-tests-and-tropes/the-eulogy-of-damocles). The setting is bleak so that the choice to care about a doomed village means something.

That effect requires the players to be invested, and investment requires safety. A player who fears the Game Director rather than the world is not immersed; they are defending themselves. Nothing you can inflict on a character produces a better story than a player who trusts you enough to let their character be hurt.

The world may be merciless. You are not the world.
