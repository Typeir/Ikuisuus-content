# The Contract of Mechanics

A rule is a promise. When a player reads that a [# kw:Repose #] sheds one level of [# kw:condition:exhaustion #], they are not learning a fact about the world — they are being told what they can count on. Every plan they make afterwards is built on that promise.

Break the promise quietly and you do not just change an outcome. You remove the ground the players were standing on, and there is no way for them to tell that it happened. Rules are the only part of the world the players can inspect directly. Everything else is your word.

---

## What the Contract Says

Three clauses, and they bind you rather than the players:

1. **The rules mean what they say.** If a curse is permanent, it is permanent. If a Raw Roll ignores magical modifiers, it ignores them for the villain too.
2. **The rules apply symmetrically.** A monster and a character in the same situation get the same adjudication. Asymmetry that exists is written down — that is what a stat block is for.
3. **Changes are announced before they matter.** A house rule declared at session zero is a rule. The same change introduced after a roll has been made is a reversal.

---

## Do Not Fudge

The temptation arrives in a specific shape: a character is about to die, the death is anticlimactic, and the die is behind your screen. Nobody would know.

Do not.

Not for a moral reason — because of what it costs mechanically. Damocles builds its tension out of scarcity: ten-day [# kw:Recovery #] cycles, [Grievous Wounds](/en/library/rules/life-blood-and-ruin/grievous-wounds) that outlast the fight, [Deeds, Grit, and Misdeeds](/en/library/rules/steel-and-strife/deeds-grit-and-misdeeds) that can be overspent. Every one of those systems only produces tension if the players believe the numbers are load-bearing. A single saved character costs you nothing visible and devalues every subsequent roll at the table, including the ones you wanted to matter.

If a death would be anticlimactic, the error was upstream: the encounter was misjudged, or the stakes were never declared. Fix it upstream. See [Coherent Stakes](/en/library/rules/tales-tests-and-tropes/coherent-stakes).

The honest tools are all available to you before the dice land — do not call for the roll, set a different DC, offer a cost instead of a consequence, let the enemy have a reason to take prisoners. Use those. Once the die is cast, read it.

---

## Roll in the Open, or Do Not Roll

Open dice are not a moral position, they are a maintenance cost. Rolling where the table can see removes the possibility of fudging, which removes the temptation, which removes the suspicion. Whatever you decide, decide it once and hold it for the campaign.

The same applies to DCs. Announcing the target before the roll — "this is a 20" — costs nothing and turns a die roll into a decision. See [Tests, Trials, and Tribulations](/en/library/rules/tales-tests-and-tropes/tests-trials-and-tribulations) for when a roll is warranted at all.

---

## Rulings Are Not Rule Changes

You will hit situations the rules do not cover. Rule on them immediately, briefly, and in the direction that keeps play moving. That is not a breach of contract; that is the contract working.

It becomes a breach when the same situation is ruled differently the second time. Keep a list. Three lines in a notebook is enough:

- What came up.
- How you ruled.
- Whether it stands.

Bring the list to the table when it grows. Ratified rulings become house rules, and house rules are promises like any other.

---

## When You Get It Wrong

You will misremember a rule, apply the wrong DC, forget a resistance. Say so plainly, at the moment you notice.

- If the error is still live, correct it and continue.
- If it resolved several scenes ago, let it stand and correct the rule going forward. Unpicking an old outcome costs more trust than the error did.

Either way, name it out loud. A Game Director who visibly corrects themselves is a Game Director whose uncorrected calls the table can believe.
